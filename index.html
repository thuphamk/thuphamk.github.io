Career Journey

To build a tall house, the foundation has to be solid.

I started earning money early, with nothing grand: renting out comics I'd already read, writing poems on request, and short stories for the school wall newspaper.

When I started working, I also began with the smallest jobs. At times I was the one running back and forth, keeping all sorts of things going in a production site of more than a hectare worth tens of millions of dollars, at my first company.

Back then I didn't think any of it would help my career. If there was work, I did it; if there was something to learn, I learned it; if I didn't know, I asked. Then from logistics, commercial, marketing and product development to managing teams and running a company, each stage gave me another way of seeing the business.

Looking back, I see that those seemingly unrelated jobs shaped how I work today: understanding the small details without forgetting the big picture; caring about each department while always asking how they fit together.

After one month of operating, I saw the original positioning had to change, and that pulled in the product range, sourcing, warehouse, sales and customer care.

Read the full story

In 2013, Mason Bees started with the idea of “Fresh and Organic Food!”. The business operated in the clean and organic food space in Vietnam. With the original positioning and limited supply, the store focused on about 100 SKUs, mostly vegetables, root crops and fruit.

After a month, the market made me realise the positioning needed adjusting. It wasn't only about brand or marketing: the product range, sourcing, warehouse, sales and customer care all had to be reviewed to fit the new direction. The team and I adjusted these links so the expansion wouldn't turn into disconnected activities.

The range then grew from about 100 to more than 5,000 SKUs, and average revenue per store grew about 10 times across a chain of 3 stores. The business also successfully raised its first round from an angel investor.

Lesson: a strategy has to be flexible enough to change when reality speaks, and whoever runs the business has to see how that change touches everything else.

When I took over, the whole marketing team had left, the data was wiped and revenue was falling. The first job was finding the root problem in each department.

Read the full story

I love beauty, but I'm rather lazy about beauty routines. So the first challenge at Thien My wasn't just a new job; it was proving to the investor that I could solve the business problem they were facing.

When I took over, the entire marketing team had left, data had been deleted, revenue had dropped sharply and was still falling; there was disagreement internally and operations were unstable. I started by collecting the data that was left, analysing the situation and fixing the foundation issues in each department. From pricing and the service menu to marketing and operations, each part was adjusted and then joined into a more unified process. In parallel, I recruited and rebuilt the marketing team.

After that, revenue at one point reached close to 3× the level at takeover, and profit doubled.

Lesson: when a business is in trouble, the most visible problem isn't always the root one. Sometimes the job isn't to push one department to run faster but to help the whole system fit together.

03
Tami Nature Home

From a contract factory to a consumer-facing brand.

10,000+mouth-spray units in the first 3 months on e-commerce platforms

Tami started as a cosmetics factory doing OEM/ODM. My partners and I brought its own brand closer to consumers.

Read the full story

Tami Nature Home started from a chemical-cosmetics factory doing OEM/ODM. When I began working with them, the investor wanted to build their own brand and bring their strongest products from wholesale closer to consumers.

The original cosmetics lines didn't really suit tastes in the big cities. My partners and I repositioned the brand, redesigned the packaging, reviewed the range and dropped slow-moving lines, and built e-commerce alongside the traditional channel.

Market research led us to a mouth spray for Gen Z: energetic, distinctive, handy every day and easy to carry. From design and packaging to pricing and sales policy, everything was set for that customer. It sold more than 10,000 units in its first 3 months on e-commerce platforms.

Lesson: building a brand isn't only making the product look better; sometimes the range, the target customer and the way you take it to market all have to change.

ASPO UNIVERSE

Entering a new market, where marketing and product shape the experience together.

Top 10App Store, RPG downloads
18,000+player accounts

GameFi was still new when I joined ASPO UNIVERSE, a Gosu Corporation project. Marketing here wasn't only promoting a finished product; it helped shape the experience.

Read the full story

Online games are popular in Vietnam, but GameFi was still quite new when I joined ASPO UNIVERSE, a project under Gosu Corporation. The field had potential but a lot of uncertainty; I chose to try it in an environment where you build and learn at the same time.

With the marketing team, I built the gamer community and worked with the development team on characters, storyline and gameplay. I also planned, budgeted and ran online and offline activities and in-game events.

The app reached the App Store Top 10 for downloads in the role-playing games category, with more than 10,000 downloads in the first week; later the platform recorded more than 18,000 accounts and about 7,000 daily active users. The team kept at least 3 community activities a month.

Lesson: with a digital product, marketing has to work alongside the development team to create the experience, build the community and keep users engaged.

05
Saigon Security

Redesigning how a business runs.

100%tender win rate across the tenders entered

Security services have thin margins and heavy staffing costs. I looked at the problem through how the whole company is organised, not only revenue or cost.

Read the full story

Security is a low-margin industry with heavy staffing costs, and it has to keep service quality while staying competitive. Running Saigon Security, I looked at this not only through revenue or cost but through how the whole business is organised and run.

I revised company regulations, operating procedures, the org chart and job descriptions. Pay is tied flexibly to revenue, balancing the company's competitiveness with employees' income and livelihoods. I also changed how service cost is calculated and the pay policy for security staff, the people who directly create the quality of the service.

On business development, I took part directly in tenders for projects of large corporations, with a 100% win rate across the tenders I entered.

Lesson: running a business well comes from seeing how structure, process, cost, pay policy and competitiveness connect, rather than optimising each part on its own.
